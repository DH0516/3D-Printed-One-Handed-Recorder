# 2. Background & Inspiration

Although recorders are commonly misconceptualized as simple instruments, they are acoustically sophisticated wind instruments. This misconception largely stems from the mid-20th century, when music educators (including the Hungarian composer Zoltán Kodály and others) successfully introduced the recorder into elementary school classrooms as an accessible starter instrument. In reality, the fipple (mouthpiece) and jet mechanisms rely on complex fluid dynamics to split air and produce sound, requiring high design precision. This historical and modern background highlights the engineering challenges and inspirations behind the development of one-handed woodwinds.

### 2.1 The Tabor Pipe: An Early One-Handed Fipple Woodwind
Historically, playing a woodwind instrument with a single hand has precedent in the **tabor pipe**, a three-holed fipple flute dating back to the Middle Ages and Renaissance. Designed to be played with only one hand, it allowed the musician to simultaneously play a small drum (the tabor) with their free hand. By using three holes (two on the front, one on the back) and utilizing overblowing techniques to access higher overtones, players could cover a wide playing notes range.

### 2.2 The Dolmetsch recorder & Expired Mechanics
While the tabor pipe uses a limited number of tone holes and overblowing, playing a fully chromatic, standard-range recorder with one hand typically requires a key mechanism. Since the 1930s, the Dolmetsch family has crafted custom recorders for individuals with hand accessibility needs. In the 1990s, this design was further developed into a model that could be produced in greater volumes, inspired by Brenda Dickeson.

The Dolmetsch design allows a player to use only one hand (with left- or right-handed variants) by replacing the finger holes of the missing hand with a specialized 4-key mechanism. This system incorporates a two-position thumbkey (closed/open) operated by the little finger of the player's active hand, mapping the notes normally played by the other hand onto keys.

The technical mechanics of the original Dolmetsch recorder-keywork are documented in historical patent records dating from the **1950s**:

| Patent | Subject | Grant Date |
|---|---|---|
| **GB852164** | Side-mounted closed key operated by the little finger | 8 June 1959 |
| **GB852165** | Bell key | 8 June 1959 |
| **GB852135** | Echo / "piano" key | 1958 |
| **GB666602** | "Tone-projector" | 1950s |

Based on standard term limits, these patents expired in the late 20th century. Public database searches of Espacenet, Google Patents, and the USPTO for any later custom designs (including the ~1997 volume model) did not reveal active patent protection on the basic one-handed key layout. Because all patents covering these historical mechanical keywork designs have expired, reproducing, modifying, or using these mechanics does not violate any patent rights, placing the mechanics in the public domain.

Out of these historical patents, the One-Handed Recorder project only utilizes the side-mounted key operated by the little finger (GB852164). Other wind instruments commonly utilize a pinky key mechanism as well, making the specific novelty of this historical recorder patent unclear; regardless, all of these patents have since expired. In addition to this layout, the project also provides a thumb-operated version of this key to enable a stronger, more reliable seal of the octave hole, as the pinky-operated key can sometimes be prone to seal leakage.

Additionally, the key mechanisms used in this recorder are mechanically no different from the standard keywork used in common Boehm-system woodwinds (such as modern flutes, clarinets, and oboes). They rely on the same fundamental mechanical principles (including pivot hinge tubes, spring returns, and padded cups to seal tone holes) which have been standard, open-domain woodwind engineering concepts for over a century.

### 2.3 The Rise of 3D Printed Musical Instruments
3D printing musical instruments has gained interest in the maker community, supported by resources from organizations like **Prusa Research** (such as their YouTube video ["3D print a Recorder, an Ocarina or a Kazoo"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tMuK4TjFtgI) and accompanying [blog guide](https://blog.prusa3d.com/3d-print-a-recorder-an-ocarina-or-a-kazoo-3d-pritned-musical-instruments_49390/)). These developments highlight how consumer-grade FDM printers can be configured to produce the acoustic geometries of a recorder. To differentiate from closed or proprietary designs, this project provides a free and open-source (FOSS/FOSH), customizable, FDM 3D-printable framework for one-handed recorders.

### 2.4 The "Flow" Project
In recent years, the intersection of music education and assistive technology has led to projects like **Flow**. Developed as an inclusive, low-cost, 3D-printed one-handed recorder, Flow aimed to provide school students with physical disabilities a way to participate in music education. The Flow project demonstrated the application of additive manufacturing to create functional assistive musical instruments.

### 2.5 Historical Prior Art & Other Makers
The concept of one-handed recorders has been independently explored and implemented, reinforcing that the general approach is well-established prior art:
- **Tsukamoto & Fujikoka (1980):** Developed one-handed soprano and alto recorders by adapting Zen-On plastic recorders, documented in the article *"A One-Handed Recorder"* in *Recorder and Music Magazine*.
