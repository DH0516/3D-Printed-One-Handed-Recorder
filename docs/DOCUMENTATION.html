1. Overview

The 3D Printed (FDM) One-Handed Recorder is an open-source project aimed at creating an accessible, fully functional musical instrument. It is inspired by the Dolmetsch one-handed recorder series, which was developed to accommodate musicians with accessibility issues in coordination of their arms, fingers, or hand. It is not a completely hands-free solution, but it provides a starting base for a fully acoustic instrument that can be adapted to fit a player's hands while maintaining playing quality. This is a purely acoustic instrument that does not involve electrical components.

This project focuses on creating a reproducible, open-source design optimized for consumer-grade FDM 3D printing. We utilize standard FDM printing and readily accessible off-the-shelf parts to reproduce the specialized key system required for one-handed play. While the core concepts and design ideas remain the intellectual property of the author/team (distributed under an open-source non-commercial license such as the Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial CC BY-NC, which requires that this project is cited when used), users have the freedom to print, modify, and customize the designs for their personal physical needs. Selling the design files or physical instruments for commercial profit is strictly prohibited.

2. Background & Inspiration

Although recorders are unfortunately often misconceptualized as "simple" instruments, they are acoustically sophisticated wind instruments. This misconception largely stems from the mid-20th century, when child music educators (including Zoltán Kodály and others) introduced the recorder into elementary school classrooms as an accessible starter instrument, after being forgotten for a long time after the late Baroque period. In reality, the fipple (jet oscillator) relies on complex nonlinear fluid dynamics to split air and produce sound, requiring high design precision. This historical and modern background highlights the engineering challenges and inspirations behind the development of one-handed woodwinds.

2.1 Tracking back on the Fipple: An Early One-Handed Woodwind

Historically, playing a woodwind instrument with a single hand has precedent in the tabor pipe, a three-holed fipple flute dating back to the Middle Ages and Renaissance. Designed to be played with only one hand, it allowed the musician to simultaneously play a small drum (the tabor) with their free hand. By using three holes (two on the front, one on the back) and utilizing overblowing techniques to access higher overtones, players could cover a wide playing notes range. This project continues in spirit of these tabor pipes in introducing single-handed fipple woodwind in the performer's arsenal.

2.2 The Dolmetsch recorder & Expired Mechanics

Since the 1930s, the Dolmetsch family has crafted custom recorders for individuals with hand accessibility needs.

The Dolmetsch design allows a player to use only one hand (with left- or right-handed variants) by replacing the finger holes of the missing hand with a specialized 4-key mechanism. This system incorporates a two-position thumbkey (closed/open) operated by the little finger of the player's active hand, mapping the notes normally played by the other hand onto keys.

The technical mechanics of the original Dolmetsch recorder-keywork are documented in historical patent records dating from the 1950s:

Patent Subject Grant Date
GB852164 Side-mounted closed key operated by the little finger 8 June 1959
GB852165 Bell key 8 June 1959
GB852135 Echo / "piano" key 1958
GB666602 "Tone-projector" 1950s

Based on standard term limits, these patents expired in the late 20th century. Public database searches of Espacenet, Google Patents, and the USPTO for any later custom designs (including the ~1997 volume model) did not reveal active patent protection on the basic one-handed key layout. Because all patents covering these historical mechanical keywork designs have expired, reproducing, modifying, or using these mechanics does not violate any patent rights, placing the mechanics in the public domain.

Out of these historical patents, the One-Handed Recorder project only utilizes the side-mounted key operated by the little finger (GB852164). Other wind instruments commonly utilize a pinky key mechanism as well, making the specific novelty of this historical recorder patent unclear; regardless, all of these patents have since expired. The project's first models (A1 and A2) provide a thumb-operated version of the octave key to enable a stronger, more reliable seal of the octave hole, as the pinky-operated key can sometimes be prone to seal leakage. However, an alternative design that maps one-to-one mapping of the keys and also avoiding the thumb usage went back to making this design to be considered again. Without an actual precise knowledge of what this Dolmetsch system is, if this potentially becomes a dispute of "this is my proprietary design", it will be pulled out. Otherwise, all the designs of this project are going to be FREE and OPEN-SOURCED, near FOSS, except commercial reselling of the design for profit, or claiming the intellectual property ownership AFTER this project has become available. The colleagues that are working on this project are actively working to make sure there is a publication to prevent such potential foul play.

Additionally, the key mechanisms used in this recorder are mechanically no different from the standard keywork used in common Boehm-system woodwinds (such as modern flutes, clarinets, and oboes). They rely on the same fundamental mechanical principles (including pivot hinge tubes, spring returns, and padded cups to seal tone holes) which have been standard, open-domain woodwind engineering concepts for almost two centuries.

2.3 The Rise of 3D Printed Musical Instruments

3D printing musical instruments has gained interest in the maker community, supported by resources from organizations like Prusa Research (such as their YouTube video "3D print a Recorder, an Ocarina or a Kazoo" and accompanying blog guide). These developments highlight how consumer-grade FDM printers can be configured to produce the acoustic geometries of a recorder. To differentiate from closed or proprietary designs, this project provides a free and open-source (FOSS/FOSH), customizable, FDM 3D-printable framework for one-handed recorders.

2.4 The "Flow" Project

In recent years, the intersection of music education and assistive technology has led to projects like Flow. Developed as an inclusive, low-cost, 3D-printed one-handed recorder, Flow aimed to provide school students with physical disabilities a way to participate in music education. The Flow project demonstrated the application of additive manufacturing to create functional assistive musical instruments.

2.5 Historical Prior Art & Other Makers

The concept of one-handed recorders has been independently explored and implemented, reinforcing that the general approach is well-established prior art:

  • Tsukamoto & Fujikoka (1980): Developed one-handed soprano and alto recorders by adapting Zen-On plastic recorders, documented in the article "A One-Handed Recorder" in Recorder and Music Magazine.

3. Project and Instrument Details and Contexts

3.1 Target Audience

  • Individuals with accessibility needs of the upper limbs with some limitations (one hand, or two hands with partial limitations).
  • Organizations working with such individuals, wanting to provide a starting point for a solution.
  • Performers looking for a double-soprano-recorder solution.
  • Researchers and educators interested in assistive musical technology.

3.2 Instrument Details

  • Type: Currently only Descant (Soprano) variant. Others will become available if enough users request them.
  • Key System: Four keys replace the missing hand's fingers and thumb.
  • Fingering: Requires a specialized fingering chart, which is an inversion of the holes to keys, where keys replace the holes. Left-hand and right-hand models use mirrored variants of the fingering system.
  • Handedness: The primary instrument design (v1) is right-handed. A left-handed instrument can be made by mirroring everything horizontally along the axis of the bore.

3.3 Development Realities

  • Prototyping vs. Mass Production: For this project, the advantage of 3D printing is rapid prototyping and customizability rather than mass production. General mass production forces standardization which challenges the basic fact that every human is different (not just your handspan).
  • Development Process: Creating a functional acoustic instrument with 3D printing involves significant calibration, testing, and fine-tuning of acoustic tolerances.
  • Iterative Adjustment: Print calibration and trial-and-error are expected parts of the process, and designs may need adjustments to fit individual printer tolerances.
  • The Key Challenge: Mechanical keys have been a major focus in our initial research. Because keys require airtight padding and precise pivots, we collaborated with a professional instrument repairer for direct consultations.
  • Iterative Redesign: We went through numerous redesign cycles to achieve reliable key seals and responsive movement.
  • Our Goal: Our goal is to empower users and local makers to fabricate and iterate on these designs themselves using accessible consumer tools.
  • Core Purpose is "Accessibility": The project is designed to serve upper-limb accessibility, utilizing consumer-grade FDM printers and common, safe FDM thermoplastics.
  • Access and Upgrade Barriers: While mass-produced plastic soprano recorders are widely available and affordable, upgrading to wooden instruments or obtaining customized parts is costly and difficult. The inherent sophistication of the recorder makes it challenging to customize, particularly due to the complexity of the fipple, but also extending to key layouts and custom sizing.
  • No Proprietary Hardware: Any non-printed hardware additions (e.g., rubber bands, springs, pads, pins) must be readily purchasable by the general public at standard hardware stores or online retailers. No proprietary or custom-engineered hardware is required. If a specific part is not available, we will suggest alternative items, and users are welcome to contact us for suggestions.
  • Freedom to Print & Modify: Users are encouraged to print, replicate, test, and modify the files to fit their personal anatomical requirements.
  • Academic Licensing & IP Attribution: While users have freedom to print and modify the designs, the core design ideas and research development belong to the original author/team, protected under an open-source non-commercial license (such as Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial CC BY-NC). This allows anyone to print, share, and modify the designs for personal or educational use, but strictly prohibits commercial sale or exploitation of the design files or finished instruments for money, and requires that the original project and author/team are cited when the design is used or shared.

3.4 Materials Selection & UV Resin Safety Hazards

  • We know many materials out there: When designing a 3D-printable instrument, there are numerous options available, ranging from liquid photopolymers to various meltable thermoplastics.
  • Toxicity of Consumer-Grade UV Resin: Standard consumer-grade photopolymer UV resins used in stereolithography (SLA/DLP) printers present severe health and safety risks, making them unsuitable for mouth-contact instruments like recorders. In their liquid state, these resins contain reactive monomers, oligomers, and photoinitiators that are known skin and respiratory irritants. Direct contact can cause severe allergic contact dermatitis and chemical sensitization over time (Macdonald et al., 2016; Alifui-Segbaya et al., 2017).
  • Incomplete Curing in Opaque Resins: If non-translucent (opaque) UV resin is used, it is highly likely that the inside of the printed parts was not cured properly because UV light cannot reach there. Opaque pigments (like Titanium Dioxide or Carbon Black) absorb and scatter UV light, reducing the cure depth (Tomec et al., 2022). This leaves toxic, uncured liquid resin trapped within the internal structures of the instrument, posing severe contact health hazards.
  • FDM with PLA Selection: Due to these safety concerns, we had to choose FDM (Fused Deposition Modeling) with PLA (Polylactic Acid) in the end as our main baseline (though other FDM materials like PETG should work fine too). Unless using certified biocompatible medical/dental-grade resins subjected to strict medical curing procedures, SLA resins must be avoided for mouth-contact instruments.

3.5 Design Integrity & Intellectual Property Compliance

To ensure respect for intellectual property while keeping our work completely open-source, the project operates under a set of design integrity and compliance guidelines:

  • Independent Expressive Creation: All CAD models, photographs, instructions, and text in this repository are created independently by the team.
  • Factual Data Re-derivation: The fingering layout and acoustic measurements are independently compiled and re-tabulated.
  • Trademark Boundaries: We market this project under its descriptive name, 3D-Printed-One-Handed-Recorder. Historical names (such as "Dolmetsch") are referenced purely descriptively and historically to identify the design inspiration, never as a product logo or trademark.
  • Academic Licensing & IP Attribution: As described in Section 3.3, the project utilizes the Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial (CC BY-NC) license. This permits free non-commercial recreation, customization, and study of the design files, provided that the original project and author/team are cited when shared or used.

3.6 Fipple Mechanics & Head Joint Exclusion

At this stage, this project does not include design files for printing the head joint (mouthpiece) of the recorder. This decision is based on two primary considerations:

  • Design and Acoustic Complexity: The fipple and jet mechanisms of the recorder are highly complex and are subjects of popular acoustics studies. In particular, the labium is a very complicated construct, requiring precise sizing of all four sides and the internals. A poorly designed or printed labium causes severe sound defects, commonly observed in low-quality recorders. To avoid providing a low-quality instrument, we recommend using a generic, easily available commercial head joint that plays well.
  • Safety and Liability: We are hesitant to instruct users to place home-printed objects directly into their mouths. Even if the materials used are certified as safe, the project team cannot assume responsibility or liability for any health concerns that may arise from using home-fabricated mouthpieces.

4. How to 3D Print

4.1 What is Needed (Tools & Materials)

Materials:

  • Filament: Standard 1.75 mm PLA or PETG. Warping materials like ABS and ASA have not been tested at all, and we never recommend using photopolymer resin, especially for any mouth-contact part; see Project and Instrument Details and Contexts.
  • Commercial Recorder Head Joint: A standard 440-442 Hz plastic soprano recorder head joint is required for the soprano-440 model, as the head joint is not printed in this project. (See Fipple Mechanics & Head Joint Exclusion for details).
  • 1.75 mm Filament Snippets: A few short pieces of ordinary printer filament. One acts as the pivot pin for Key 1; the others are the bending pin springs that close Keys 2 to 4.

  • Sealing Pads: Discs of 1.0-1.5 mm closed-cell neoprene, natural cork, craft foam, or soft leather, cut to the key cup diameters. This is the only part of the instrument where it cannot be "3D printed".

  • Tenon Seal: Paper tape, thread, O-ring, or cork for the friction joints.

Tools:

  • 3D Printer: Any consumer FDM printer with at least 160 mm of Z height. The middle joint is the tallest part at roughly 15 to 16 cm.
  • Finishing Tools: 400 to 1200 grit wet/dry sandpaper, miniature needle files, and deburring tools to clean up tone holes and clear away print stringing hair if you experience stringing issues.
  • Pliers & Tweezers: Needle-nose pliers, fine tweezers, flush cutters, or a small craft knife.

4.2 What Settings to Use (Suggested Recommendations)

[!NOTE] Most generic or general slicing settings should be okay for a standard print. The fine settings detailed below are recommended from a troubleshooting perspective to optimize print quality, airtightness, and acoustic performance.

  • Calibration & Tolerances: Ensure your printer is properly calibrated for dimensional precision before printing. The instrument design is optimized so that standard tolerances of ~0.5 mm are generally okay.
  • Orientation: Print the body and foot joint vertically along the bore axis with the tenon on the build plate. Never print the acoustic body horizontally: horizontal layer lines cause bore distortion and air leakage. Print the keys with standard slicer supports.
  • Layer Height: 0.12 mm is recommended for better acoustic seal and detail on tone hole bevels; 0.16 mm is acceptable.
  • Wall Perimeters: Use at least 4 to 6 wall loops (minimum 2.0 mm total wall thickness) to ensure complete airtightness through layer lines and provide structural rigidity.
  • Infill: Printing as a full solid (100% infill) is ideal, but if you are experiencing over extrusion issues, you could print with sparse infill, while keeping the infill density high (80%+). The sparse infill pattern itself does not affect the sound; choose a simple pattern (such as grid or rectilinear) to save print time and ensure complete sealing around the bore.
  • Bridges: Disable "thick internal bridges" and "detect overhang walls" in your slicer settings to prevent internal bore distortions. (Unless you have a specific way to handle these).
  • Supports: Try to not add supports on the tone holes, as supported holes can come out dirtier and rougher than expected. Supports are needed for the keys, tenons, and key holders. I prefer tree supports for easier detachment.
  • Seams: Random seams provide stronger seals for the bore. Avoid using scarf seams as they can introduce leaks.
  • Brim: Use an outer brim of at least 5 mm to ensure solid bed adhesion when printing tall joint sections vertically.

4.3 Pitch Precision & Tone Hole Resizing

Because 3D printing involves material shrinkage, and slicing variations, the printed instrument's acoustic pitches might not be perfectly precise out of the box.

Generally speaking, adjusting the tone holes are not advised. You can consider cleaning the holes and the bore if the print produced stringings, but further changes are not necessary. There are compensatory shapes around tone holes to offset the overhang sagging. Please consider reprinting after checking your calibrations and measurements rather than jumping to making manual changes on the printed instrument.

5. How to Assemble

No screws and no bought springs: the keys snap into the pillar slots on the body, and short snippets of 1.75 mm printer filament form the pins and the springs. Tools and materials are listed in How to 3D Print.

Step 1: Clean and Inspect Printed Parts

  • Remove all slicer support material from the keys.
  • Check the internal bore of the body and foot joint. Clear away any strings, burrs, or loose filament bits so the air column is unobstructed.
  • Inspect each tone hole rim. Ensure the rims are clean, flat, and free of burrs; lightly touch them with fine sandpaper if needed so the key pads seat completely flat.
  • Lightly sand the male tenons and female sockets with 400-grit sandpaper until parts test-fit smoothly without binding.
  • Optionally apply a thin coat of food-grade oil (flax, or similar used in woodwind oiling) to the internal bore for improved sealing.

Step 2: Prepare and Glue Sealing Pads

  • Cut or punch pad discs to the key cup diameter (pad materials are listed in chapter 4).
  • Place a small drop of glue in each pad cup, press the pad disc in flat and level, and let the glue cure fully before mounting the keys.

Step 3: Mount Keys 2 to 4

  • Align each key's bracket with the open slots in its pillar pair on the body and press it down until it snaps securely into place.
  • Test each key: it should turn smoothly on its pivot without binding or excessive wobble.

Step 4: Install the Filament Pin Springs (Keys 2 to 4)

  • Insert a straight snippet of 1.75 mm filament through the pillar posts and pin holes of each key so that it acts as a pin spring.
  • Rotate the filament pin in the direction that applies its appropriate spring force against the shaft's hook.
  • Snap off the filament at the notch so the pin spring stays in one rotational position.
  • Press each touchpiece with your finger: the pad should lift smoothly, and when you release, the pin spring should snap the pad shut instantly over the tone hole rim.

Step 5: Mount Key 1 (Octave Lever)

  • Position Key 1 into its pin slot on the body. The sheet spring is already 3D printed with it.
  • Cut a straight snippet of 1.75 mm filament to the width of the pin slot and slide it through the pin slot holes and Key 1, creating a clean pivot pin. Trim any excess spring.
  • Key 1's return spring is the flexible printed sheet spring, printed as part of the key itself; it keeps the octave hole closed at rest until the touchpiece is pressed.

Step 6: Fit the Joints

  • Wrap both tenons of the body with paper tape, thread, an O-ring, or cork (all work fine) until the head joint and the foot joint each slide on with a firm, airtight friction fit. A light touch of cork grease helps.
  • Push on the head joint and the foot joint, and visually align the front tone holes with the labium window on the head joint so the instrument is straight.

Step 7: Perform Airtightness and Playability Checks

  • Gently blow warm air through the recorder while keeping all tone holes closed.
  • Listen and feel for air leaks around the pad cups. If a pad is not sealing completely, check that the pad sits flat in its cup and that the pin spring provides enough closing force.
  • Test finger action: press each touchpiece to ensure full, unobstructed travel and snappy spring return.
  • Play through the notes of the fingering chart.

If you need pre-cut tenon grooves for thread or O-ring seals, or you modified the design and want to share it, write to the contact address on the main page.

6. Troubleshooting

6.1 Sound & Acoustic Issues

  • Issue: The recorder sounds "airy", weak, or fails to speak on low notes (Low C, C#, D).

  • Cause 1: Inadequate Key Pad Seal. Inspect each key pad using a flashlight placed inside the bore in a darkened room or test with a narrow paper feeler gauge. If light leaks around the pad rim, re-seat or sand the tone hole chimney flat.

  • Cause 2: Tenon Joint Leakage. Air escaping at the head-to-body or body-to-foot joint weakens acoustic pressure. Add extra layers of thread, PTFE tape, or cork wrapping to achieve an airtight seal.

  • Cause 3: Leaky Print Walls. If improperly printed, air can bleed through micro-gaps between layer lines, usually happens around toneholes. Ensure at least 4 perimeter walls were used, or seal the interior bore with a thin coat of mineral oil or paraffin wax. Sparse Infill patterns likely will not change the acoustic outcome.

  • Issue: Lower register sounds weak compared to upper, or constantly overblows.

  • Cause 1: Octave Thumb Hole or Key Leak. The thumb hole key (Key 1) or other keyed hole must seal completely when fully closed and provide a clean aperture when vented.

  • Cause 2: Leaky Print Walls.

6.2 Mechanical & Keywork Issues

  • Issue: Keys stick, bind, or are sluggish to return.

  • Cause 1: Pivot Friction. The pivot hole in the key hinge may be too tight. Gently sand or ream the hole until the key turns freely.

  • Cause 2: Insufficient Spring Tension. Increase the bending preload on the filament pin spring, or replace the snippet with a fresh one.

  • Cause 3: Key Arm Clearance. Check that key arms do not rub against adjacent pillar posts or the outer curvature of the body.

  • Issue: Key pads fall off or shift out of alignment.

  • Solution: Clean the key cup thoroughly with isopropyl alcohol before gluing. Use gel cyanoacrylate or contact cement, ensuring full surface contact and allowing adequate cure time under clamping pressure.

6.3 Joint Fit & Physical Tolerances

  • Issue: Joints are too tight to assemble or bind during insertion.

  • Solution: Do not force printed joints together. Apply less thread/cork wrapping. If the bare printed tenon is oversized, wet-sand the outer diameter with 400-grit sandpaper until it inserts smoothly. Then apply cork grease or wax.

  • Issue: Joints are loose or wobble during play.

  • Solution: Add more tenon wrap until the connection is snug, rigid, and airtight.

7. Model Information

The project supports multiple variants of the same one-handed soprano recorder. All variants share one fingering chart (see the fingering viewer and the machine-readable model data in models/).

  • A1, Soprano: the standard model, described by the Model A1 documentation. The thumb key operates similar to the "A" key, to be pushed with the joint of the index finger, sideways. This particular design involves avoiding the usage of the thumb completely. Right-handed by default; the design is based on a resizing of a historical instrument by J.H. Rottenburgh (originally estimated to be pitched at A = 405 to 415 Hz) to modern pitch (A = 440 Hz). For left-handed play, the design must be flipped horizontally (mirrored along the bore axis). * Note for organology specialists: We can likely produce custom model designs based on different historical models upon request.
  • A2: A1 with key 1, the octave key, moved to the back of the body as a thumb key.

8. Reference Tools & Resources

  • Interactive Fingering Chart Viewer: A dedicated web application to view and practice the fingerings, laid out for the one-handed mechanism. The chart itself is the standard baroque chart ("English" and not the "German" system), since the keys change which finger works a hole rather than which holes are open.
  • Features: Three note-naming modes (Simple, Solfège, Standard); Simple and Solfège number the instrument's own octaves (C1 to C#3, Do 1 to Di 3), Standard keeps scientific names (C5 to C#7). The playing range is chosen at a reasonable random for people who would require this when learning the instrument as a beginner. Staff notes are drawn one octave lower to match the instrument's register. Plus search filtering and print/PDF of the current view.
  • Standalone Use: Fully static; it reads the model list and each model.json from the models/ directory, so serve the folder on any static host (GitHub Pages included) and open it from there.
  • Model A1 Documentation: Specifications and print files for the standard soprano build; printing, assembly, and troubleshooting are in chapters 4 to 6.
  • Acoustic Modeling: Inria Openwind, the open-source wind instrument design toolbox used to calculate and optimize the bore and toneholes.
  • Design Compliance and History: Historical background on one-handed woodwinds (including expired patents) can be found in Background & Inspiration. Open-source licensing and IP compliance details are listed in Project and Instrument Details and Contexts.
  • Community Support: In these early stages of release, we welcome educators, makers, and families reaching out to us for assembly advice, troubleshooting, and collaborative feedback.
